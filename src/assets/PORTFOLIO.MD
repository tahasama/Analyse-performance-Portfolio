# Portfolio Governance Document

*Reference rules for building and rebuilding the portfolio. Part 1 governs what gets disclosed and in what order. Part 2 governs how it looks. Both apply together — visual design must reinforce disclosure order, never override it.*

---

# PART 1 — Portfolio Positioning & IP Disclosure Rule

## 1. Primary portfolio objective

The portfolio is primarily a showcase of:

> **Performance Systems for Document Control**

The six concrete products/capabilities are the main portfolio work and must remain the visual and narrative focus.

The portfolio should communicate:

> **"I design performance systems for Document Control."**

It should NOT primarily communicate:

> "I build generic software/systems."

The author's document-control expertise is the foundation. Analytics, reporting, systems thinking, and governance architecture are differentiators built on top of that domain expertise.

---

## 2. The six products are the portfolio hero

The first layer of the portfolio must focus on:

1. Status & Visibility
2. Flow & Performance
3. Findings & Advisory
4. Process & Action
5. Strategy & Impact
6. Commitment & Tracking

Each should be presented as a concrete professional capability / product with:

- the problem
- what was built
- what it delivers
- why it matters
- visual evidence

The visitor should understand these six products without knowing DCIOM terminology.

Do NOT require the visitor to understand:

- pillars
- handshakes
- principles
- C1–C6 terminology
- DCIOM
- the DCIOM Standard
- the DBoK

before understanding the six products.

---

## 3. Do not turn the portfolio into a "framework presentation"

The portfolio must NOT drift into:

> "Here is my governance framework."

The primary message is:

> **"Here are six performance systems I designed for Document Control."**

The architecture explains how those systems can connect, but it is not the product being sold first.

The framework should therefore be revealed after the practical work has been understood.

---

## 4. Three levels of disclosure

Use three levels of information.

### Level 1 — Public / primary

Show openly:

- six products
- screenshots / visual evidence
- business problems
- product purpose
- high-level functionality
- outputs
- practical value
- broad system relationship
- high-level architecture

This is the recruiter-facing portfolio content.

### Level 2 — Public / secondary

Introduce, but summarize:

**DCIOM.** Describe it as:

> **An independently designed documentation-specific performance-governance framework.**

It is acceptable to show at a high level:

- that the six products form a broader architecture
- the three broad pillars
- the six-stage conceptual flow
- the existence of governance principles
- the existence of formal interfaces between parts of the system

Do not expose the detailed mechanics.

**DBoK.** Describe it as:

> **An independently authored architectural reference mapping the wider documentation and information-governance landscape.**

Show:

- breadth
- chapter structure
- disciplines covered
- research scope

Do not reproduce the book's detailed intellectual architecture.

### Level 3 — Deep / private or optional

Do NOT put the following detailed information into the normal public portfolio:

**DCIOM:**

- exact handshake rules
- exact data-crossing rules
- exact escalation logic
- exact thresholds
- exact Health Score weights/formula
- exact metric formulas where they constitute the method
- detailed routing rules
- detailed decision-ownership rules
- simulation equations
- model assumptions
- correction / dispute mechanics
- implementation-specific governance logic

**DBoK:**

- detailed conceptual architecture
- detailed boundary logic between disciplines
- full relationship model
- extensive framework decomposition
- detailed proprietary synthesis / methodology
- full book content

The public portfolio should demonstrate that the author has this depth without publishing the entire blueprint.

### 4.1 Visual evidence handling rule (practical addition)

The six product pages must not expose exact formulas or internal governance thresholds — even if a current screenshot happens to reveal some of them. Crop, abstract, or redesign the visual whenever necessary rather than accidentally publishing the blueprint. This matters specifically because the underlying Standard contains exact formulas, thresholds, handshakes, ownership logic, and simulation mechanics, and any of that leaking through an unedited screenshot would violate the Level 3 boundary above.

---

## 5. Recommended portfolio narrative

The portfolio should progressively reveal depth:

**Stage A — What I built.** Six products.

**Stage B — How the products connect.** A very simple conceptual flow:

> **Observe → Measure → Diagnose → Act → Project → Verify**

This should be understandable without DCIOM terminology.

**Stage C — Architecture.** Introduce:

> **Document Management → Reporting → Decision**

Explain briefly that the six products were developed within a broader governance architecture.

**Stage D — Framework Design.** Introduce DCIOM as:

> **the independently designed framework behind the six systems.**

Show only high-level architecture.

**Stage E — Research.** Introduce DBoK as:

> **the broader architectural / research work that maps the documentation profession and its surrounding governance disciplines.**

Show breadth, not the full intellectual blueprint.

---

## 6. The visitor's intended understanding

The portfolio should create this progression:

**First impression** — "This person understands Document Control."

**After seeing the six projects** — "This person can build serious reporting and performance systems around Document Control."

**After seeing the architecture** — "This person thinks at a systems / governance level."

**After seeing DCIOM** — "This person has designed a formal methodology around that thinking."

**After seeing DBoK** — "This person has broader conceptual and research depth beyond the immediate project."

The visitor should NOT initially think:

> "This is a person presenting an unestablished standard and asking me to learn it."

---

## 7. IP / originality rule

The fact that DCIOM and DBoK are self-authored and not externally established does NOT mean they should be hidden.

They should be presented as:

> **independent professional / research work**

rather than:

> **industry standards**
> **certified frameworks**
> **widely adopted methodologies**
> **universally recognized models**

The portfolio should create interest in the deeper architecture without publishing every mechanism required to reproduce it.

---

## 8. Design implication

The visual hierarchy must reflect this positioning.

**The six product pages should receive:**

- the strongest visuals
- the richest screenshots
- the clearest problem/solution storytelling
- the highest visual prominence

**The Architecture page should be:**

- elegant
- concise
- conceptual
- visually strong
- lower in implementation detail

**The Research page should:**

- communicate breadth
- show the DBoK structure
- show intellectual depth
- avoid reproducing the full book

Do not allow the Architecture or Research pages to visually dominate the six concrete projects.

---

## 9. Important distinction

The portfolio is NOT saying:

> **"I invented performance governance."**

It is saying:

> **"I designed documentation-specific performance systems and formalized the architecture behind them."**

The individual ideas may draw on established practices and management concepts.

The value of the author's work is the combination of:

- hands-on Document Control experience
- EDMS knowledge
- analytics / Power BI / DAX
- systems development
- governance thinking
- independent framework design

---

## 10. Final rule for the rebuild

When choosing between:

**A.** showing more architectural detail, and
**B.** showing more evidence of the six products,

choose **B** for the main portfolio.

The portfolio should leave the visitor thinking:

> **"This person builds performance systems for Document Control."**

Then, for visitors who want to understand the deeper intellectual work:

> **"There is a whole architecture behind it."**

That architecture is available as a deeper layer, not required knowledge on entry.

---

# PART 2 — Visual Design Direction — Editorial Enterprise Systems

## 1. Overall visual concept

Do NOT choose a single pure visual theme such as:

- "classic paper portfolio"
- "dashboard / SaaS portfolio"
- "corporate consulting website"

Instead, use a **hybrid editorial + enterprise-systems aesthetic**.

The overall design should feel like:

> **Editorial technical publication + enterprise information system + professional case-study portfolio**

The design must communicate two sides of the author's work:

**Operational / systems side:** document control, EDMS, dashboards, KPIs, workflows, reporting, analytical outputs, governance systems.

**Intellectual / architectural side:** framework design, system architecture, DCIOM, DBoK, research, governance models, principles.

These two sides should feel like parts of the same professional identity.

---

## 2. Visual hierarchy of the portfolio

The visual style should evolve as the visitor goes deeper.

**Layer 1 — Products / Work.** Use a **dark technical / systems-oriented visual mode**. This is the most important layer. The visitor should immediately feel: "These are real systems / products built for Document Control."

Use: dark navy / charcoal background, high contrast, strong screenshots, data visualizations, structured panels, technical labels, restrained interface-like elements.

Do NOT make it look like a generic SaaS dashboard. The dashboards are evidence of the work, not the identity of the entire website.

**Layer 2 — How the products connect.** Use a **transition between systems and editorial design**. Show a simple conceptual flow:

> **Observe → Measure → Diagnose → Act → Project → Verify**

This should be visually simple and understandable without knowing DCIOM. This page/section should feel like a bridge: practical systems → underlying architecture. Do not expose detailed framework mechanics here.

**Layer 3 — Architecture / DCIOM.** Move toward a more **editorial / technical-standard aesthetic**.

Use: warm off-white surfaces, dark text, serif headings, thin rules, numbered sections, restrained diagrams, architecture grids, annotated relationships.

The feeling should be: "This is the thinking and architecture behind the systems." It should feel more like a serious technical framework than a dashboard. Do not expose every implementation rule, threshold, formula, routing rule, or simulation equation on the public page.

**Layer 4 — Research / DBoK.** This should be the most **editorial / publication-like** section.

Use: warm off-white / paper-like surfaces, dark text, strong serif typography, chapter numbering, clean reference-style hierarchy, restrained diagrams, editorial spacing.

The Research page should feel like a serious professional reference work rather than another software product page. Use the DBoK chapter map to demonstrate breadth. Do not reproduce the full intellectual architecture or full book content.

**Layer 5 — Experience.** Use a hybrid approach. Experience should visually connect: practical professional background, systems work, analytical capability, architecture / research depth.

Use: structured timeline, restrained metrics / badges, skills grouped by domain, selected evidence, contact information. It should feel like the professional proof behind everything shown earlier.

---

## 3. Theme transition

The visual transition should be intentional:

Home / Six Products — **Dark → technical → operational**
↓
Connection / Flow — **Dark + editorial transition**
↓
Architecture — **Mixed → structured → technical/editorial**
↓
DCIOM — **Mostly editorial / technical standard**
↓
DBoK / Research — **Mostly editorial / publication**
↓
Experience — **Hybrid**

This creates a narrative:

> **I build systems → I understand how they connect → I design the architecture → I research the profession.**

Do not use the transition as decoration. It must support the narrative.

---

## 4. "Artifacts" as a visual motif

Treat each of the six products as a professional **artifact / system output**.

Examples: Status & Visibility → Status Report / Operational State; Flow & Performance → Performance Report; Findings & Advisory → Findings & Advisory; Process & Action → Quarterly Performance Assessment; Strategy & Impact → Strategy & Impact / Simulation; Commitment & Tracking → Meeting Pack / Working Worksheet.

The artifact should appear as a deliberate designed object inside the portfolio, not as a random screenshot pasted onto a page.

Use: framing, controlled spacing, subtle shadow or matte, caption, metadata, consistent evidence treatment.

The visual should communicate: "This is something I built and used." — not: "Here is a screenshot."

---

## 5. Color system

Use a restrained palette.

**Foundation:** deep navy / charcoal, warm off-white, muted slate for secondary text.

**Accent:** restrained gold / brass. Use gold for key labels, important architecture markers, selected navigation states, important calls to attention. Do NOT use strong gold everywhere.

**Data / analytics accent:** one cool blue only, for charts, analytical visuals, data moments, system-state highlights. Do NOT assign a different color to every component. The six components should look like **one coherent system**, not six unrelated products.

**Status colors:** use only where they carry semantic meaning — healthy, attention, critical, resolved, stagnant, deteriorating, missed / at risk. Status colors should never be decorative.

---

## 6. Typography

Use a two-font hierarchy.

**Serif** — for hero statements, major section titles, editorial statements, architecture / research headings. Purpose: create intellectual authority and document/reference character.

**Sans-serif** — for navigation, metadata, UI, metrics, dashboard content, captions, labels, technical descriptions. Purpose: create precision and system readability.

The combination should visually connect **document / publication** with **system / interface**.

---

## 7. Layout principles

Prefer: large whitespace, strong alignment, consistent grid, thin dividers, strong hierarchy, restrained card use, large visual evidence, clear section labels, numbered systems, clear reading order.

Avoid: excessive rounded cards, excessive drop shadows, decorative gradients, stock images, icon walls, excessive animations, rainbow component colors, fake SaaS UI, ornamental diagrams with no informational function.

The site must look **designed**, not decorated.

---

## 8. Do not let the six products become six different visual themes

All six project pages share one visual system. Differentiate them through their **visual behavior**, not through separate color palettes.

- Status & Visibility — Snapshot / counters / pipeline
- Flow & Performance — KPIs / charts / trends / breakdown
- Findings & Advisory — Ranked findings / severity / constraint / advisory
- Process & Action — Quarter comparison / movement / conclusion / escalation
- Strategy & Impact — Trajectory / scenarios / intervention / horizon
- Commitment & Tracking — Meeting record / owner / target / next-cycle continuity

Each product should therefore have its own **visual grammar** while remaining part of one design language.

---

## 9. System continuity motif

Use a subtle visual system line across the portfolio:

> **01 STATUS → 02 PERFORMANCE → 03 FINDINGS → 04 ACTION → 05 IMPACT → 06 COMMITMENT**

The current component can be highlighted when on its page. This line is a portfolio navigation / narrative device.

Do NOT initially label it C1 → C2 → C3 → C4 → C5 → C6. Use plain-language names on the public-facing portfolio. Later, the Architecture page may reveal that these correspond to the formal DCIOM components.

---

## 10. Architecture reveal

On the Architecture page, reuse the same visual language introduced on the product pages. The visitor should recognize that the six products they just saw are the practical expression of a deeper architecture.

Use the same system line / flow motif. Then introduce the formal structure gradually: six capabilities → conceptual flow → three pillars → formal handshakes → principles → DCIOM.

Do not present everything simultaneously in the first frame.

---

## 11. Research reveal

The DBoK should feel like a natural extension of the architecture.

Use: chapter index, structured chapter map, editorial typography, reference-style navigation, selected concepts, breadth visualization.

The purpose of the Research page is to show the breadth and depth of the author's thinking — it is NOT to make recruiters read the DBoK. Provide enough evidence to make the work credible, with optional links to deeper material.

---

## 12. Responsive behavior

The visual system must survive desktop, laptop, tablet, and mobile.

Do not build architecture diagrams that depend on fixed absolute coordinates. Prefer CSS grid, flex, responsive cards, stacked relationships on mobile, semantic HTML.

Diagrams should collapse into readable vertical sequences rather than becoming tiny unreadable graphics.

---

## 13. Professional tone

The visual language should feel: serious, mature, technical, intelligent, restrained, credible, modern.

It should NOT feel: flashy, futuristic for the sake of it, startup/SaaS, academic in a dry way, corporate-template generic, over-designed.

The intended impression is:

> **"This person understands information, systems, data and governance at the same time."**

---

## 14. Most important design rule

The portfolio must visually reinforce the following hierarchy:

> **The six systems are the work.**
> **The architecture is the depth behind the work.**
> **DCIOM is the framework that formalizes that architecture.**
> **DBoK is the broader intellectual/research evidence.**

Do not reverse this hierarchy. Do not let the Architecture or Research pages dominate the six product pages.

The visitor should first see **what was built**, then discover **how it was designed**, and finally **how deeply the author thinks about the profession.**
